Defining it

What financial wellbeing is

Financial wellbeing is not the same as having money. It is how secure you feel, and how well your money supports the life you want.

Definition
“A state of being wherein a person can fully meet current and ongoing financial obligations, can feel secure in their financial future, and is able to make choices that allow enjoyment of life.”

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, Financial Well-Being: The Goal of Financial Education (2015)

ControlOver day-to-day and month-to-month finances
ResilienceCapacity to absorb a financial shock
ProgressOn track to meet your financial goals
FreedomAble to make choices that let you enjoy life

Researchers Netemeyer, Lynch and colleagues (2018) measure the same idea in two parts: current money management stress and expected future financial security.

Financial security

Your finances are off your mind

Bills are covered, a surprise will not sink you, and you feel confident about the future. This is peace of mind.

Two kinds of wellbeing: how you feel today, and how you rate your life

Research on money measures wellbeing in two ways, and money does not affect them equally. Happiness is an emotion that comes and goes; life satisfaction is a lasting judgment. Financial wellbeing is linked to both.

Life satisfaction

How you judge your life overall

A considered evaluation when you step back, often asked as a rating of your life from 0 to 10. Security, progress and a sense of control shape it.

With income: keeps rising, with no plateau found. This is where money shows up most clearly.

Daily emotions

How you feel from moment to moment

Joy, calm, stress, worry and sadness, measured by asking about yesterday or checking in during the day. This is what research usually means by happiness.

With income: improves for most people too, but less than you might expect. The exception is the unhappiest fifth of people: their daily emotions stop improving above about $100,000, likely because more money cannot fix causes like grief or depression.

The key: money on your mind costs you joy

It is not money itself that dulls enjoyment. It is thinking about it. In one study, simply seeing a picture of money made people savor a piece of chocolate for less time and enjoy it less (Quoidbach and colleagues, 2010). Thinking about the cost while you enjoy something has a similar effect, like a ticking taxi meter that takes the pleasure out of the ride (Prelec and Loewenstein, 1998).

That is the thread running through the ten ways. A cushion, money on autopilot and paying ahead for what you enjoy all keep money off your mind, so you can be present for the parts of life it pays for.

For workplaces

Where workplaces can make an impact

Money worries do not stay at home. They show up at work as distraction, stress and lost performance. Employers shape many of the systems behind financial wellbeing: the defaults people start with, the benefits they can reach, the time they have and how their work is designed.
